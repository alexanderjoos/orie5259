# PBV Strategy Train of Thought

The PBV idea started from a simple execution question: instead of buying mechanically at the start of each minute like TWAP, can we use the evolving size in the order book to wait for a slightly better moment inside the minute? The first version, `pbv.ipynb`, looked directly at how the top 5 bid and ask sizes changed over time within each minute. I initially thought the second derivative would be the key signal, because it seemed like a natural way to detect when visible pressure was accelerating or fading. That gave a clean first prototype and showed that pure top-of-book timing was too weak, but that using more of the book helped.

From there, the next step was to make the representation more meaningful. In `pbv_v2_size.ipynb`, the raw levels were grouped into pressure buckets like near ask, far ask, near bid, far bid, and midpoint imbalance. That made the strategy easier to interpret and more general across stocks. It also showed an important tradeoff: richer composite signals sometimes looked better conceptually, but they became too selective and often fired too late or relied on fallback execution. So the lesson from that stage was that feature design and execution logic had to be balanced; adding complexity was not automatically improving the actual trading rule.

`pbv_v3.ipynb` was really the simplification pass. After seeing the composite approach get too rigid, I moved back toward a cleaner hand-built rule that kept the bucket framework but focused on the strongest pieces of information. At that point it started to look like the cumulative state of the book and the first-order movement were more stable than the pure d2-heavy version. So even though the project started around second derivatives, the iteration process naturally pushed toward the idea that the state of the queue mattered more than just curvature by itself.

The `v4` notebooks were the shift from hand-crafted triggers to learned scoring. In `pbv_v4_logistic.ipynb`, I asked whether the PBV buckets should be treated as model features rather than as direct threshold signals. That improved the prediction side, but it exposed a new issue: a model can classify whether a point is better than the minute open without necessarily choosing the best execution point in the minute. So the bottleneck stopped being just “which features should I use?” and became “what exactly am I training for, and how should that prediction be turned into an execution decision?”

That is where the imbalance extensions came in. In `pbv_v4_logistic_imbalance.ipynb`, I tried to bring in the Lecture 8 idea that visible size may be more or less informative depending on imbalance and hidden liquidity. The first attempt added imbalance across all levels, but that ended up overlapping too much with the top-of-book information already captured by PBV. In `pbv_v4_logistic_imbalance_deep.ipynb`, I corrected that by focusing on deeper levels only, which made the imbalance feature more complementary. That felt like the right conceptual direction, even if it still did not fully solve the execution problem.

The last step in the current chain was `pbv_v4_argmax.ipynb`, which was less about introducing a final strategy and more about diagnosing what was still wrong. By then, the main realization was that the remaining issue was probably the decision rule itself. If the model is scoring many ticks within a minute, then firing at the first threshold crossing may be the wrong use of that information. So the end of this iteration path is really a transition point: the PBV idea looks strong enough to keep, the bucket representation seems useful, deeper imbalance seems more sensible than naive imbalance, but the next improvement should come from better intra-minute selection logic rather than just continuing to tune the same d2 signals.

For Milestone 1, that is really the main reason to keep PBV as one of the retained strategies: it evolved from a simple intuition into a much clearer execution framework, and each version taught us something specific about what matters. The final takeaway is not that one notebook solved the problem completely, but that this line of work consistently pointed toward a useful combination of bucket-based state, simpler features, and smarter intra-minute selection, which makes it one of the most credible strategies to carry forward.
